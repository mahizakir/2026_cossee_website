01From the Centre

This is the first issue of a monthly note to the lab. The idea is simple. One place, once a month, for the things that are easy to miss when you are deep in a screening spreadsheet or manuscript writing: what moved in the field, who has joined us, what we published, what happened here, and which deadlines are about to arrive.

One item is worth reading even if you read nothing else: Culina and colleagues have made the case that risk-of-bias assessment belongs at the centre of ecological synthesis, and their survey numbers are sobering. §04 has the detail.

This is meant to be useful to you rather than to me, so tell me what belongs in it. If there is a topic you think the lab should be reading about (a method, a debate, a resource, a recurring question in your own work), send it over or add it to the intake doc and it will go in the next issue. Suggestions about what to drop are just as welcome.

One housekeeping note: the abbreviations used throughout are spelled out in a list at the end, and every abbreviation is linked to its entry the first time it appears in a section.

04In the field

Verified developments from outside the lab over the period, with a note on why each one matters here.

Synthesis & open science
COSSEE co-authored

Risk-of-bias assessment is barely practised in ecology, and the paper has the numbers

“Elevating the importance of Risk of Bias assessment for ecology and evolutionary biology” · Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 13 July 2026 · Culina, Foster, Grainger, O'Dea, Sánchez-Tójar, Pescott, Boyd, Koricheva, Nakagawa & Stewart · 10.1111/2041-210X.70356

Of 232 researchers with evidence-synthesis experience surveyed, only 28 were familiar with risk-of-bias assessment, 46 confused it with publication bias, and just 10 had ever performed one. Of 275 EEB journals reviewed, 209 look likely to solicit evidence syntheses; only five of those referenced any guidelines for conducting one, let alone for assessing risk of bias.

The paper proposes making risk-of-bias assessment a condition of credible synthesis via journal policy, improving how primary studies report their methods, and building field-specific risk-of-bias tools with training to go with them, plus four questions every synthesist should answer. Expect this to be cited alongside PRISMA-EcoEvo, and expect reviewers to start asking.

Data sharing in ecology is largely solved. Code sharing is not.

“Data- and code-archiving in the British Ecological Society journals” · Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 2026 · Natalie Cooper & the BES Data and Code Hackathon Group (137 authors) · 10.1111/2041-210X.70338

An audit of 1,861 papers, roughly a fifth of everything published in seven British Ecological Society journals from 2017 to 2024. Of papers using data, 97% archived it. Of the 90% of papers using code, only 35% archived it. About a third had no README at all, median README quality was 7 out of 10 for data and 6 for code, and only 69% of archived data was judged complete.

The authors' framing is the useful part: accessibility has been fixed, usability is the live problem. Good benchmark if we want to write down a lab code-sharing standard.

The counter-argument, published in February: “Demonstrations of reproducibility are not reproducibility”

Nature Ecology & Evolution Correspondence 10(4):615–616, 13 Feb 2026 · Gómez-Llano & De Lisle · 10.1038/s41559-026-03000-w

Concedes that mandatory data archiving delivered real benefits, then argues that newer requirements (code that must run on any machine, extensive metadata) impose heavy costs on thin justification, and confuse demonstrating reproducibility with achieving it. Worth reading directly against the BES audit above; the two together would make a good lab meeting.

A 2019 insect-decline paper with 120 citations has been retracted

“Parallel declines in abundance of insects and insectivorous birds in Denmark over 22 years”, Ecology and Evolution 2019 · retraction reported 23 June 2026 · Retraction Watch

Anders Møller reported that, over 1,375 drives through the Danish countryside across 22 years, insect splatter on his windscreen fell by 80% on one route and 97% on another. The paper has now been retracted over data duplications and inconsistencies that the editors say invalidate its conclusions; it had 120 Web of Science citations.

The Danish Committees on Scientific Dishonesty found in 2004 that Møller had fabricated data in a 1998 Oikos paper on oak-leaf asymmetry, and he is among the most prolific contributors to the eco-evo meta-analytic literature, which makes this a provenance question for any synthesis carrying his effect sizes, not just a news item.

A reasonable habit: check the retraction status of included studies at the revision stage, not just at screening.

Reporting standards for AI in evidence synthesis are now in place, including in environmental evidence

“Position statement on artificial intelligence use in evidence synthesis across Cochrane, the Campbell Collaboration, JBI and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence” · Environmental Evidence 14:20, 31 Oct 2025 · 10.1186/s13750-025-00374-5 · CEE reporting guidance (a living document, Nakagawa a co-author)

The four organisations published their joint position simultaneously in their four journals, including Environmental Evidence, so it applies to us directly; it endorses the RAISE guidance (Responsible AI use in evidence SynthEsis).

CEE's own reporting guidance operationalises it: for every AI system used you report name, version, developer, validation references, position in the workflow, custom parameters, protocol deviations, human–AI agreement, limitations and ethics, plus supplementary files containing the exact prompts and the rationale for them.

Relevant context: Xie et al., a medRxiv preprint posted 19 March 2026, meta-analyse 18 studies and report a pooled sensitivity of 0.92 for LLM title/abstract screening. Two caveats are the preprint's own: the corpus is medical, and it has not been peer reviewed.

The third is ours: a sensitivity of 0.92 implies that roughly 8% of relevant records are missed, which by our reading is not acceptable for single-pass screening in a PRISMA-EcoEvo review. That inference is arithmetic from the reported figure, not a finding of the paper.

“Adaptive preregistration” answers the standard objection from modellers

“‘But I can't preregister my research’: improving the reproducibility and transparency of ecology and conservation with adaptive preregistration for model-based research” · Methods in Ecology and Evolution, online 30 April 2026 · Gould, Jones, Yen, Fraser et al. · 10.1111/2041-210X.70311

Two mechanisms for people who say their modelling workflow is too iterative to preregister: registered flexibility (preregistering flexible heuristics, with alternative analysis paths made contingent on earlier decision points) and interim preregistrations (versioned preregistrations at successive phases). Developed with user-centred design and tested on an environmental-flows case study. Useful if we want a preregistration norm that survives contact with real model development.

Also worth knowing: Peer Community Journal is now indexed in Web of Science, and PCI reported 521 submissions in 2025 across 21 thematic PCIs, with 123 journals now PCI-friendly, so the long-standing objection that publishing through PCI does not count for anything on your CV has weakened considerably.

And Mizuno, Williams, Lagisz, Senior & Nakagawa's April preprint showing that phylogenetic and spatial meta-analysis are the same model with a different distance metric has worked implementations in metafor, glmmTMB and brms. Handy the next time a reviewer asks about spatial autocorrelation. (preprint, 9 April 2026; PCI 2025 highlights)

Ecology & evolution

Ninety years of Swiss insect records: the steepest declines were in the 1930s–1960s

Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2 June 2026 · Neff et al. · 10.1038/s41559-026-03074-6

1.2 million records, 1930–2021, 595 saproxylic beetle and 216 butterfly species. Losses peaked during agricultural mechanisation, with beetles later partially recovering and butterflies not. The authors argue that analyses restricted to recent decades produce shifted-baseline artefacts, a temporal-coverage caveat that applies to essentially every insect-decline meta-analysis, including any we might run.

Global butterfly range shifts across 1,758 species, deliberately including non-English literature

Nature Ecology & Evolution, 5 August 2026 · Chowdhury et al. · 10.1038/s41559-026-03117-y

About 10% of known butterfly diversity across 105 countries; 80% of species showed range expansion and 79% of those shifts were linked to climate change. The methodological interest for us is the search design: literature in 15 languages, grey literature and assessments from 68 experts, explicitly to reduce geographic and language bias. A citable precedent when we argue for multilingual searches.

An experimental intervention in the fragmentation debate

Nature Ecology & Evolution, 17 June 2026 · Fletcher et al. · 10.1038/s41559-026-03095-1

27 experimental landscapes and more than 3,000 cactus patches with specialist insect herbivores. Habitat loss, fragmentation and matrix quality all had negative effects. Against the prevailing assumption, a high-quality matrix helped more in less-fragmented landscapes. Direct experimental evidence in one of the longest-running controversies that synthesis has failed to settle.

A claimed general rule of biodiversity organisation may be a mid-domain artefact

Nature Ecology & Evolution Matters Arising, 6 July 2026 · Zhang & Wang · 10.1038/s41559-026-03118-x

Shows that the reported core-to-transition gradient also emerges from randomly generated, non-biogeographical regions and from a simple mid-domain geometric effect. An unusually clean teaching case in how an analytical pipeline (here, identifying “characteristic species” by spatial dominance) can manufacture the pattern it then explains. The original authors' Reply runs alongside it.

Policy and funding worth knowing about. Banting and Vanier are gone. The tri-agencies replaced agency-specific postdoctoral fellowships and Banting with the harmonised CPRA, part of the new Canada Research Training Awards Suite. The 2024–25 round (results February 2025) was the last Banting competition.

NSERC launched the Geoffrey Hinton Prizes on 28 July 2026: up to three per year at $100,000 each for early-stage researchers applying AI in novel ways, awarded off the back of Discovery Grant applications. AI-assisted screening and extraction pipelines plausibly qualify, which makes this a funding line for methods work already underway here.

NSERC and SSHRC expanded the Indigenous Scholars Awards to doctoral ($40,000/yr) and postdoctoral ($70,000/yr) levels on 18 June 2026, with supplements of $5,000 (doctoral) and $10,000 (postdoctoral) for things like travel to northern and remote communities, childcare and eldercare.

And the revised Tri-Agency Open Access Policy is still not out. The 2015 policy remains in force, release is now anticipated “in 2026” with a transition period, so zero-embargo deposit is not yet binding. Keep accepted manuscripts deposit-ready anyway.
