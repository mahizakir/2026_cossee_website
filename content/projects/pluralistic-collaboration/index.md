Scientific disagreements rarely concern evidence alone. They also involve terminology, ethics, risk, professional roles, institutional responsibilities and expertise.

Existing approaches, however, often seek consensus or focus on testing a discrete empirical claim. Adversarial collaboration enables researchers with competing views to jointly design studies, preregister methods and interpret results. It is powerful when a disagreement can be converted into a tractable empirical test, but less suited on its own to disputes involving ethical commitments, conceptual disagreements, damaged relationships or asymmetries of authority.

Pluralistic collaboration is an emerging framework for rigorous joint work while substantive disagreement persists across empirical and normative dimensions. Pluralistic collaboration treats disagreement as multidimensional and asks participants to represent competing positions accurately before deciding what form of inquiry is appropriate. Its evaluative focus is not agreement alone, but epistemic clarity, recognition, reflective practice, working relationships and willingness to continue cooperating.
