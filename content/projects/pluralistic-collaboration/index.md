Scientific disagreements rarely concern evidence alone. They may also involve different worldviews and hoped-for futures, conflicting moral and ethical commitments, differences in risk tolerance and institutional obligations and, even different terminology.

Existing approaches, however, often seek consensus or focus on testing a discrete empirical claim. Adversarial collaboration enables researchers with competing views to jointly design studies, preregister methods and interpret results. It is powerful when a disagreement can be converted into a tractable empirical test, but less suited on its own to disputes involving ethical commitments, conceptual disagreements, damaged relationships or asymmetries of authority.

Unlike adversarial collaboration, which is typically focused on resolving empirical disputes, pluralistic collaboration explicitly engages with empirical, normative, and ontological dimensions of disagreement. Pluralistic collaboration treats disagreement as multidimensional and asks participants to represent competing positions accurately before deciding what form of inquiry is appropriate. Its evaluative focus is not agreement alone, but epistemic clarity, recognition, reflective practice, working relationships and willingness to continue cooperating.
